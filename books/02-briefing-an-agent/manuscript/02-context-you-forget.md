title: Context you forget you have

A new hire joins your support team on Monday. By Wednesday they have sent a refund to a customer on a plan that does not allow refunds. Nobody told them. Nobody told them because everyone who has been there longer than a year thinks it is obvious.

A loop is a new hire every single run. It has read a great deal and knows nothing about your Tuesday.

The hard part of briefing is not writing clearly. It is noticing what you know that is not on the page.

## Three kinds of invisible context

When you ask a colleague what they would need to know, they usually name facts. That is the visible part. The context that sinks a run tends to be quieter.

**Vocabulary.** Words that mean something specific in your office. "The Q3 list" is a spreadsheet, not a quarter. "Priority" is a field with three values. "Enterprise" means over 500 seats, except in finance, where it means the contract type. A loop will take the dictionary meaning unless you override it.

**Conventions.** The way things are done here. Dates in YYYY-MM-DD. Customer names written as they appear in the billing system, not as they sign their emails. A draft never promises a delivery date. None of these are written down anywhere. They live in the habits of people who have been corrected before.

**Constraints.** Things that are off the table, for reasons that come from history. "We do not discount in December." "Legal reads anything that mentions a competitor." "That customer is in a dispute, so no one writes to them without Dana." These are the expensive ones, because a loop cannot guess them and the person who knows them is the one who forgot to mention them.

## The stranger test

You cannot find your own blind spots by thinking harder. You find them by handing the page to someone else.

Give the draft brief to a colleague who has not worked on the job. Ask them to read it and write down every question they would ask before starting. Do not answer the questions. Do not explain. Just collect them.

Each question is a missing line. Some will be cheap: "Which spreadsheet?" Some will make you wince: "Wait, can we talk to customers who are in arrears?" Those are the lines that would have cost you something.

If you have no colleague available, read the page as someone from a different department. A person from finance reading a support brief will ask about refund authority in the first minute.

## Where to put what you find

Not everything belongs on the page. A brief is one page, and a page that grows into five is skipped.

Sort each discovered fact into one of three places.

- **In the brief** if it changes this job's result. The competitor list. The 90-day window. The destination of the output.
- **On the standing-notes shelf** if it applies to every job you run. Dates as YYYY-MM-DD. Do not name competitors in customer drafts. Keep the shelf short, and be willing to defend every line on it.
- **In a reference the loop can look up** if it is long and only sometimes relevant. The refund policy. The glossary. A list of customers in dispute. The brief names the reference in a line, and the loop reads it with a tool when the case calls for it.

This is the same sorting as the memory shelves in Book 1, used as an editing tool. The question is always: does this need to be in view on every beat, or only available when needed?

## A glossary line is cheap

If your job uses three or four words in a special way, define them in the brief itself. It takes a line each.

```
TERMS
- "Active customer": paid an invoice in the last 12 months.
- "Escalation": a ticket tagged P1 or P2 in the support tool.
- "The list": the sheet named Renewals Q4, tab "Working".
```

It looks fussy. It removes a whole class of confident, plausible, wrong answers. A model asked for "active customers" without a definition will pick one. The one it picks will not be yours.

## The constraint you have never said out loud

Constraints are worth a deliberate hunt, because they tend to be negatives, and negatives hide in people's heads.

Ask yourself these, for your job.

1. Who must never be contacted by this work, and why?
2. What would embarrass us if it appeared in the result?
3. What did we try before that failed or was rejected, so it must not be proposed again?
4. Which numbers or claims need someone's sign-off before they leave the building?
5. What is true about this customer, team, or project that changes the answer, and that only three people know?

The third one deserves emphasis. A loop will cheerfully propose the idea that was rejected last spring. It does not know it was rejected. Write the decision, with a date and an owner, in the same way Book 1 suggested: not only the idea, but the verdict. "Do not propose a goodwill refund on the Basic plan. Confirmed by the policy owner on 2026-04-02."

## When the context is a person

Some context lives in one person's head and nowhere else. You cannot move it onto the page by wanting to. You have to ask them.

If a job depends on that person's judgment, the honest brief says so. Add a line that tells the loop to stop and ask, and name the person.

```
STOP
Stop and ask Priya if a customer is flagged "at risk" and the
renewal price is below list.
```

That is not a failure of the brief. It is the brief correctly noticing where a human decision lives.

## What to do with this chapter

Take a brief you have already written, or the one you wrote at the end of the previous chapter. Give it to someone who does not know the job. Collect their questions without answering them.

Then sort each question into the brief, the standing notes, or a reference. Add a line. Run the stranger test again with a different person. When the second reader has no new questions, the page is ready to hand to a loop.
