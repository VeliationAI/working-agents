Pick up one real task. Leave the platform comparison for a week when you have a brief worth comparing against.

Choose a recurring job of the find-and-compose kind. Not the hardest problem in the company. A brief you already produce by hand. Meeting prep. A weekly note on a competitor, drawn from named sources. A summary of a support queue. A packet of notes before an interview.

Then do this once, with a clock running.

1. Write the one-page brief. Swap the Acme example for the task you actually have. If a line embarrasses you because you do not know the answer, that line is the point.
2. Run it read-only. No sending, no editing of records, no "while you are there".
3. Timebox the run. If it is not done when the box ends, stop it and read the trace.
4. Grade the handback against the tests, not against your hopes. Note every spot where you had to open a source yourself because the handback would not let you trust it.
5. Turn those spots into tests, or into a source line the handback must include.
6. Run it once more. Only then consider a single write action, and only as a draft you approve.

If you are the builder, log every beat. Goal, action, observation, check. A run you cannot replay is a run you cannot defend. Widen a tool only after a week of dull, correct drafts.

If you lead a team, the most useful meeting this week is not a vendor selection. Collect three real jobs. Write a finish line for each. Agree which actions are allowed to leave the building without a person. Write that agreement down. It will still be true after you change tools.

## What to ignore for now

Ignore the pressure to start with a team of agents. Extra agents do not repair a vague goal. They give you more traces to reconcile. One loop, with a finish line and a narrow set of tools, will teach you more in a week than a diagram of ten specialists.

Ignore leaderboards and demo videos until you have your own brief. A demo is someone else's goal, chosen because it looks good. Your brief is the exam. When you do watch a demo, ask to see the trace, the tool list, and the stop rule. You now know why those three things are the whole conversation.

Ignore the feeling that you are behind. The people who look fastest are often running moods as if they were goals. You can be slow on Monday morning, with a one-page brief, and be further along by Thursday than a team that has already sent the wrong email.

The books that follow go one shelf deeper. Briefing. Tools and permissions. Memory. Patterns for real jobs. Failure and evaluation. More than one agent. Shipping. You do not need them to use this one.

You need a goal the loop can finish. Tools no wider than the job. A check that does not worship fluency. And a stop.
