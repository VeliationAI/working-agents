title: When a standing note fights today's brief
---

You run a small consultancy. Your agent has a standing note: "Always write client emails in a formal tone, no contractions." It has worked well for a year. Today you ask for a quick, warm note to a client you have known for ten years, who just had a baby.

The agent writes: "Dear Mr. Okafor. I am writing to offer my congratulations on the arrival of your child. It is with great pleasure that I..."

You wanted two sentences and an exclamation mark. The standing note won.

Or did it? Maybe the brief won and the agent wrote something formal anyway because the note was longer, older, and more specific. You cannot tell from the output. That is the problem with contradictions in memory. They are resolved silently, by a model, in a way you did not choose.

A **contradiction**, here, means two items in view that cannot both be followed. A standing note says one thing and today's brief says another. A case file entry says a customer is on the Team plan and the billing tool says Business. The archive says seven days and the pinned rule says fourteen.

You will meet contradictions often. The question is whether you decided how they resolve, or the model did.

## Why models resolve them badly

A model does not hold a rule hierarchy unless you give it one. Left alone, it weighs what is in view by signals you do not control.

- **Specificity.** A detailed note beats a general instruction. "No contractions, formal salutation, sign-off 'Kind regards'" can outweigh "keep it warm."
- **Position.** Text near the start or the end of the context tends to count for more than the middle.
- **Repetition.** A rule stated three times beats a rule stated once.
- **Vividness.** An example with a name and a number beats an abstract preference.

None of those correlate with which instruction is more authoritative, or more recent, or yours. A stale note with a good example can beat a fresh instruction with none.

So the work is on your side: decide precedence, say it, and make conflicts visible.

## Decide the order of precedence

You can fit the whole order on a card. Here is a reasonable default. Change it if your work calls for another, and then write the change down.

1. **Hard boundaries.** Safety, legal, and permission limits. "Never send without approval." "Never include customer payment details." These beat everything, including the brief.
2. **Today's brief.** The most recent statement of what you want for this job. It beats standing notes about style and defaults.
3. **The case file.** Decisions already made on this task, unless the brief says to reopen them.
4. **Standing notes.** Preferences and defaults across tasks. They fill in what the brief does not say.
5. **The archive.** Reference material. It informs, and it never overrides an instruction.

Two details matter.

First, the order is about instructions, not facts. A billing record is evidence about the world. If the case file says Team plan and billing says Business, that is not a precedence question. That is a stale entry. Resolve facts by checking the more authoritative live source and updating the file.

Second, hard boundaries sit above the brief. If your brief says "send it to the client today" and a boundary says "nothing leaves the company without approval," the boundary holds, and the handback says so. A brief can ask for more freedom than the boundaries allow. When it does, the loop should stop and ask, not pick.

## Make the order visible to the loop

Say it in the instructions, in plain words, near the top of what the loop sees.

```
PRECEDENCE
If two instructions conflict, follow this order:
1. Hard boundaries (never send, never include card data).
2. Today's brief.
3. Decisions in the case file.
4. Standing notes.
Reference documents never override an instruction.

When a standing note conflicts with today's brief:
- Follow the brief.
- Add one line to the handback: "Standing note X was
  overridden by the brief because Y."
Do not apply both halves.
```

The last line sounds odd and is important. Without it, models often compromise: a "warm but formal" blend that satisfies neither. A compromise is a third behavior nobody asked for.

The line asking for a note in the handback matters most. An overridden standing note should be seen. If it is overridden every week, it should be edited. If it was overridden by mistake, you will catch it.

## Three kinds of conflict

Not every contradiction is the same. You handle them differently.

**An instruction against an instruction.** The consultancy example. Precedence handles it. Brief beats note. Say so, and log it.

**An instruction against a fact.** The brief says "draft the invoice reminder," and the billing tool says the invoice was paid yesterday. The agent should not send a reminder to a customer who has paid. This one does not go to precedence. It goes to the stop rule: stop and tell the person the premise has changed.

**A fact against a fact.** The case file says the customer contact is Sam. The email thread from Monday has a new contact, Lena. Resolve it by finding the source with the later date and the higher authority, and by marking the other entry superseded. If the agent cannot decide, it hands both back.

Keeping these three apart will save you from using a precedence rule on a stale fact. "The brief wins" is a good rule for instructions and a terrible rule for facts.

## Supersede, do not append

Most contradictions in memory are made by a well-meaning person adding a new sentence under an old one.

```
- Refunds over $200 need manager approval.
- Update: refunds over $500 need manager approval.
```

Both lines are in view. The first is a cleaner statement. The second reads like a note. The model may follow either, and you will not know which until a refund is wrongly approved or wrongly held.

When a note changes, change the note. Delete the old one. If you need the history, keep it somewhere the loop cannot see, such as a change log with a date and a person.

```
- Refunds over $500 need manager approval.
  Changed 2026-10-03 from $200. Owner: Dana.
```

That entry is easy to follow and easy to audit. The history is in the line, short enough to carry the reason without competing with the rule.

The same applies to case files. If a decision is reversed, rewrite the entry as "Reversed on 2026-11-02 by Dana: discount approved." Do not leave "no discount" above "discount approved" and hope the later line wins.

## The note that is too broad

Many conflicts come from a standing note that was written for a situation and worded for the world.

"Always offer a call." Fine for sales leads. Absurd as a reply to a person asking to unsubscribe.

"Never discuss pricing." Written for a public social account. Applied to a renewal brief, it blocks the one thing the job needs.

The cure is the "applies to" and "does not apply to" lines from Chapter 1. A note with a scope is a note the loop can set aside, because it can see the job is outside it. A note without a scope is a rule about everything, and it will fight you on a job you did not imagine when you wrote it.

When you find a note that keeps being overridden, do not argue with the agent. Narrow the note.

## A conflict you cannot resolve by rule

Some contradictions are real disagreements between people. Marketing's standing note says never name a competitor. Sales wants the competitive brief. The note is not stale and the brief is not wrong.

An agent should not pick. The right behavior is a stop, with a handback that names the conflict.

```
HANDBACK: stopped before drafting
Conflict: the standing note "never name competitors in
drafts" (owner: Priya) conflicts with the brief, which
asks for a comparison against Northwind.
Options: (a) draft an internal-only comparison, marked as
such; (b) draft a version without competitor names.
I need a choice. No draft was written.
```

That is the right shape. It states both sides, names the owner, offers options, and writes nothing in the meantime. If you want the loop to reach that behavior, say so in the stop rule: "If two instructions conflict and the precedence order does not settle it, stop and ask."

Do not make the loop ask about every minor tension. Style conflicts usually resolve by precedence and a line in the handback. Reserve stops for conflicts with consequences: money, commitments, anything that leaves the building.

## Test your memory for conflicts

You can find most contradictions before they bite. Read your standing notes and case files as a set, once a month, with these questions.

1. Do any two notes give different answers to the same question?
2. Is there a note whose scope is wider than the job it was written for?
3. Is there a "see also" or "update" line that should have replaced an older line?
4. If today's brief said the opposite of this note, would you want the note to win?

That last question is the useful one. If the answer is "no, the brief should win," the note is a default and should be marked as one. If the answer is "yes, the note should win," it is a boundary, and it belongs at the top of the precedence order, written as one.

A boundary and a default look the same in a list of notes. They behave very differently when they collide with a brief. Sorting them is half the work.

## A trace to look for

When you suspect a conflict in a run, read the trace for these signs.

- The draft honors a note the brief did not mention, and you cannot see why.
- The draft blends two tones, two formats, or two policies.
- A fact in the output matches the case file and not the live source.
- The handback is silent about a note you know applies.

Any of those means a contradiction was resolved without you. Add the precedence block and the handback line, run it again, and compare.
