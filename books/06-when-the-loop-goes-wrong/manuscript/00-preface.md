A customer writes to say that the reply you approved on Tuesday offered a refund the policy does not give. You have the handback. You have a trace with forty-one steps. Your manager wants to know what happened, and whether it will happen again.

This book is for that afternoon, and for the weeks after it.

It is about four jobs.

1. Reading a trace until you can point at the first step that went wrong.
2. Building a small exam from ten real tasks, so you can tell whether a change made things better or only different.
3. Choosing who grades a run: a program, a model, or a person.
4. Writing up an incident so the test lands in the next brief, instead of in somebody's memory.

There is a fifth job, and it is the one people avoid. Sometimes the right response to a bad run is to take the tools away and go back to a workflow. This book says when.

Working Agents is a free series of field guides. Each book is meant to be read in an evening and heard on a commute. The PDF and the audio carry the same words, so you can switch between them. This is Book 6, When the Loop Goes Wrong. It assumes the vocabulary of Book 1, The Agent Loop: goal, tool, check, stop, handback, trace. The terms are defined again at the back. It also leans on Book 2, Briefing an Agent, because most of what you will do after a failure is edit a brief.

## Who this is for

You supervise a system that acts. You may approve its drafts. You may own the job it does. You may have built the harness that runs it. You do not need to write code to follow this book. A trace is something you can read with a pen. An exam is a folder of ten cases. A grader is a rule you can say out loud.

If you do write code, you will find the programs in this book small. That is deliberate. The checks that matter most are the dull ones, and dull checks are cheap to write.

## What this book will not do

It will not rank models. It will not report benchmark scores. It will not promise a rate of failure, because the rate depends on your jobs, your sources, and your tolerance, and it changes when anything underneath you changes.

It will not tell you that a loop can be made safe by a longer prompt. Chapter 4 explains why that belief is expensive.

## How to read it

If you have just had a bad run, read Chapter 1 on traces and Chapter 5 on the write-up. Do those two things first, while the details are fresh. Come back for the rest.

If nothing has gone wrong yet, read Chapter 2 on the ten-task exam and Chapter 3 on graders. Build the exam before you need it. A team that has an exam on the day of the first incident is calm. A team without one starts arguing about which prompt sounds better.

If a particular job keeps failing in new ways, jump to Chapter 6. It may not be an agent job at all.

## What you will have at the end

- A reading order for any trace, so the first look takes minutes.
- An exam of ten tasks drawn from your own work, with a pass table you can rerun.
- A grader specification for each test, naming who or what applies it.
- A one-page incident write-up template, and one filled in on a real run.
- A rule for deciding when a job should stop being a loop.
